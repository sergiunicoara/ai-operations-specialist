# Speaker notes — timed for 3–4 minutes (team presentation segment)

Assumes the audience has already had a short intro to the VW scandal from a
prior speaker (who is set up to introduce the case will follow with the
investigation/legal outcome). Pace: ~130 words/minute conversational.

---

### [0:00–0:45] Slide 1 — Setup

"So before we get to what VW did on purpose, I want to show you how anyone
even found out something was wrong — because it wasn't a whistleblower, and it
wasn't a regulator running a sting. It was a university research group.

In 2014, West Virginia University was hired by a nonprofit called the ICCT —
their actual goal was to *promote* clean diesel technology in the U.S. by
demonstrating it worked well on the road, not to catch anyone cheating. They
took three diesel cars, strapped portable emissions monitors to them, and
drove them around California on five different real-world routes. They also
ran the same cars through the official lab certification test, so they'd have
a direct comparison."

### [0:45–2:15] Slide 2 — The chart

"Here's what they found. [Advance to chart.] This is NOx emissions —
nitrogen oxides, the pollutant tied to smog and respiratory illness — in
grams per kilometer. This red line is the legal limit: 0.043 grams per
kilometer.

On the left, the lab test — both cars passed, comfortably under the line.

But look at the road results. Same cars. This blue vehicle — it has one type
of emissions control — measured between 15 and 35 times the legal limit,
depending on the route. This orange vehicle, different emissions technology,
measured 5 to 20 times the limit.

And I want to point at this green one, because it matters: that's a third
vehicle, a different implementation of the same emissions technology as the
orange one, and it stayed right around the legal limit almost everywhere. So
this isn't 'diesel cars just get dirty on real roads' — it's specific to how
individual vehicles were built."

### [2:15–3:15] Slide 3 — Why this can happen

"So how do you pass a test in the lab and fail it completely on the road with
the same car? [Advance to diagram.] The lab cycle is a fixed, published
speed pattern — the same every time, known in advance. Real-world driving
never repeats. If a vehicle's control system can tell the difference between
the two, it can legitimately behave differently in each — and both
measurements can be individually accurate. That's the mechanism. It's
important to say: this study alone didn't prove *why* that gap existed. The
researchers floated some innocent explanations — things like exhaust filter
cleaning cycles. They didn't accuse anyone of cheating. They just published a
number that didn't add up."

### [3:15–3:45] Handoff

"And that's the key thing I want you to take away before [next speaker] gets
into what the investigation actually found: independent, boring, methodical
measurement — not insider information — is what put this on regulators'
radar in the first place. What turns a discrepancy into a scandal is what
comes next."

---

**Total: ~3:45.** Cuttable if running long: the "green vehicle" callout in
Slide 2 (saves ~15s), or shorten the lab-cycle explanation in Slide 3 to one
sentence (saves ~20s).
