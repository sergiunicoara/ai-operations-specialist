## Slide 1 — What was measured? (0:00–1:15)

Building on the claim we have just heard, my section asks how independent measurements tested it.

In 2014, West Virginia University carried out an emissions study commissioned by the International Council on Clean Transportation. The researchers measured nitrogen oxides, or NOx, a group of air pollutants, in grams per kilometre travelled.

In the laboratory, a chassis dynamometer, a set of rollers, lets a stationary car follow a prescribed driving cycle. Here that cycle is FTP-75, the standard U.S. laboratory sequence. The study tested Vehicles A and B on it.

On the road, the researchers used a portable emissions measurement system, or PEMS: equipment carried by the car to measure its exhaust while it moves. Three vehicles drove selected routes, although not every vehicle ran every route.

The benchmark is the Tier 2 Bin 5 NOx standard: 0.043 grams per kilometre at full useful life. It is a certification standard used as a reference; a road route is not itself the legal test.

## Slide 2 — What did the data show? (1:15–2:50)

Now look at the chart. Blue is Vehicle A, orange is B, and green is C. These are the report’s anonymous labels; the EPA later identified the two high emitters as a 2012 Jetta and a 2013 Passat, without saying which letter is which. The vertical scale is NOx in grams per kilometre. The dashed red line marks the standard.

At the left, the study’s laboratory averages are 0.022 for A and 0.016 for B, both below that reference. Vehicle C has no laboratory result here. On the road, A reaches 1.505 grams per kilometre on the rural hill route. Dividing that by 0.043 gives 35 times the standard.

The report summarises A as about 15 to 35 times the standard and B as about 5 to 20 times, depending on the route. Those ranges compare road emissions with the standard. They are not road-to-laboratory ratios or single observations.

The green vehicle also matters. Its rural-route average is 0.409, roughly 9.5 times the standard, while its other route means are much lower. It provides useful context, but the vehicles differ, so it is not a controlled experiment. Error bars show the reported standard deviations, meaning variation around the average. Gaps mean no test result, not zero emissions.

## Slide 3 — What did the discrepancy mean? (2:50–3:55)

The road data justified investigation because the gap was large and appeared across several routes. But road data alone did not prove intent, identify software, or show how common the behaviour was.

That evidence came later. The EPA’s 2015 notice described a “switch”: software that read steering-wheel position, speed, engine run time and barometric pressure to recognise the official test, ran a compliant calibration in the lab, and weakened emissions control on the road. In its 2017 plea agreement, Volkswagen admitted designing that software and concealing it from regulators.

So the research exposed an anomaly, and later evidence established deliberate manipulation. The line was not crossed in 2014, when the data looked bad. It was crossed years earlier, when the car was engineered to recognise the test. That is where the investigation picks up.
