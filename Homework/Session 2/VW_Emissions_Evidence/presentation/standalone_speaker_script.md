## Opening — before Slide 1 (not tied to a timed slide; deliver over the cover slide or before advancing to it)

In September 2015, the EPA accused Volkswagen of installing illegal software in diesel cars to cheat emissions tests. By January 2017, VW had pleaded guilty to three federal felonies and agreed to pay $4.3 billion in criminal and civil penalties, covering about 585,000 affected vehicles in the United States alone — VW has separately acknowledged a much larger number of affected vehicles worldwide, but that global figure is outside the U.S. court documents this talk draws on, so I will stick to the U.S. numbers we can cite precisely. That much is widely known, and it is not where this talk starts.

What is less widely known is how the world found out in the first place — and that story does not start with a whistleblower or a regulator's raid. It starts with a small, unglamorous university research project in 2014, whose only job was to measure how much nitrogen oxide came out of three diesel cars' tailpipes on ordinary roads. Nobody involved was looking for fraud. They found a number that did not add up, published it, and went on to their next project. It took EPA and California regulators over a year of follow-up questioning, and VW engineers' own internal admissions, to turn that anomaly into a criminal case.

This talk follows that evidence trail in order, because the order matters. I am going to show you exactly what was measured, in what units, under what conditions — the raw material. Then I will show you how a single, completely accurate laboratory number became misleading once you asked what it was standing in for. Then I will show you how independent road testing exposed the gap, and only after that, how investigators established that the gap was not an accident but a deliberate design choice. By the end we will have answered three questions directly: what Volkswagen did to the test, what it was trying to achieve by doing it, and who ultimately paid for that decision. I am deliberately keeping the 2014 measurement evidence separate from the 2015–2017 legal findings throughout, because collapsing them together is exactly the kind of shortcut this case should teach us not to take.

## Slide 2 — The claim and the measurement (1:00–2:10)

NOx means nitrogen oxides, a group of air pollutants produced during combustion. Grams per kilometre is a mass-per-distance unit: it tells us how much NOx leaves the exhaust for each kilometre travelled. The benchmark in the original report is the U.S. Tier 2 Bin 5 NOx standard, 0.043 grams per kilometre at full useful life. Full useful life is the regulatory durability period. It does not mean the researchers followed each car for its entire lifetime.

The later admitted facts describe clean-diesel marketing and a strategy to sell vehicles in the U.S. The measurement mattered because buyers and regulators needed to know whether the claimed emissions performance held beyond the conditions used to obtain approval. The study therefore asked a practical question: what did these vehicles emit when researchers took the instruments onto the road?

## Slide 3 — Laboratory and road measurements (2:10–3:25)

A chassis dynamometer is a set of rollers that lets a car run through a driving sequence while staying inside a laboratory. FTP-75 is the standardised U.S. driving sequence used for the laboratory averages shown later. The road measurement used PEMS, a portable emissions measurement system. The equipment travelled with the car and measured exhaust gases and flow, alongside information about the journey. Combining pollutant mass with distance gives grams per kilometre.

Road conditions vary. An uphill stretch can increase engine load. Traffic changes acceleration and stopping. Those differences are legitimate reasons to expect some variation between laboratory and road measurements. A gap therefore needs explanation, rather than an immediate accusation.

There are two comparisons to keep separate. Comparing a road average with 0.043 asks how it relates to the standard. Comparing it with that same vehicle’s laboratory average asks how much the measured behaviour changed between conditions. The denominators differ, so the ratios differ too.

## Slide 6 — The denominator changes the meaning (6:15–7:45)

Let us work through one number. For Vehicle A on the rural hill route, 1.505 divided by 0.043 is 35.0: the road mean relative to the standard. Divide the same 1.505 by A’s own laboratory average of 0.022 and you get about 68: a road-to-laboratory ratio. Same measurement, different denominator, different headline.

That laboratory average comes from only two tests, 0.022 plus or minus 0.006, so treat 68 as an order of magnitude. The laboratory figure also includes a cold start, while the road runs were warm; the report notes that cold starts usually raise NOx, so this does not inflate the road side.

Later documents use other figures: the plea agreement says up to about 40 times for the study vehicles, which matches A’s single highest run, 1.72 divided by 0.043. ## Slide 8 — What VW did to the test (8:50–10:05)

So what exactly did Volkswagen do to the test? The later investigation supplied the mechanism that the road study could not.

According to the EPA notice, software in the engine computer, which the EPA called the “switch”, read inputs such as steering-wheel position, vehicle speed, how long the engine had been running and barometric pressure. Those inputs track the official U.S. test procedure. When the switch recognised the test, the car ran a “dyno calibration” with full emissions control. The rest of the time it ran a “road calibration” that reduced the effectiveness of its exhaust treatment.

Volkswagen’s admitted facts say the software recognised the published drive cycles, and that in 2013 a supervisor approved adding steering-angle recognition, because a car on test rollers does not steer.

So Volkswagen did not falsify a spreadsheet. It made the measured object change its behaviour when measured. The laboratory reading could be accurate and still misrepresent ordinary driving.

## Slide 9 — Where the line was crossed (10:05–11:30)

Where exactly was the line crossed? Not in 2014, when the numbers looked bad, and not in how anyone drew a chart.

According to Volkswagen’s admitted facts, around 2006 engineers concluded they could not build a diesel engine that met the stricter U.S. NOx standards and still attracted enough customers. Instead of changing the engine, they built software to detect and defeat the test. When staff objected, a senior supervisor decided to continue and told them, in substance, not to get caught. That is the line: deliberately engineering the product so that the official measurement would not represent normal use.

From 2009, about 585,000 U.S. vehicles were sold with defeat software. In 2013 it was refined with steering-angle recognition. After the 2014 study, Volkswagen gave regulators false explanations, such as driving patterns and technical issues, and a recall that the EPA says brought only limited benefit. On 3 September 2015, with its 2016 models awaiting certification, Volkswagen admitted the defeat device. The EPA notice followed on 18 September.

Every step after 2006 was concealment of a decision already made.

## Slide 11 — Why this crossed into manipulation (12:45–13:50)

Selective presentation means choosing favourable observations and omitting less favourable ones. It can mislead even when the individual numbers are correct. The documented Volkswagen conduct went further: the vehicle’s behaviour changed when the software recognised the test, and that mechanism was concealed.

The certification result could be an accurate reading of exhaust under the tested settings. The deception lay in making that conditional result stand for a vehicle whose emissions controls operated differently during ordinary use, while withholding the reason.

The broader analytics lesson is to distinguish data accuracy from the validity of the conclusion drawn from it. A number can be accurate, yet the process that generates it can make the intended comparison misleading. Independent testing helped expose that weakness, and the later investigation established why it existed.

## Slide 12 — What a skeptical analyst should check (13:50–15:05)

A skeptical analyst would start with the meaning of the number. Is it grams per kilometre, a peak, an average, or a ratio? What is the denominator? Our example changed from 35 to 68 simply by changing the comparison.

Next comes coverage. Which vehicles ran which routes? Are missing tests visible? Do repeated measurements establish consistency, and what do they leave uncertain? The chart should expose these limitations instead of quietly replacing missing values with zero.

Then check independence. Validate the instrument, vary realistic operating conditions and investigate whether a system can recognise the standard test. If the gap persists, request an explanation that makes predictions which independent tests can challenge.

Finally, separate causation from the anomaly. Exhaust measurements can show a serious discrepancy. Software evidence, documented decisions and admissions answer the question of deliberate manipulation.

The conclusion is simple: independent road measurement made the hidden discrepancy visible. It justified further investigation. The later evidence established intent. Keeping those steps distinct gives us a stronger argument, and a more trustworthy way to evaluate the next impressive test result.

## Closing — after the last slide (not tied to a timed slide; deliver after Slide 14, or over it while it stays on screen)

So where does this leave us? The measurement side of the story is finished: three vehicles, five routes, one laboratory cycle, and a gap between them that WVU's researchers could describe but not explain. The legal side is also finished, on paper — Volkswagen's guilty plea, its penalties, and its compliance monitor are matters of public record. But two things carry forward from here.

The first is regulatory, and I will flag it as background rather than a sourced claim, since it sits outside the three documents this talk is built on: real-world, on-road emissions testing — the same PEMS methodology used in the original WVU study — is widely reported to have become part of vehicle certification requirements in major markets in the years that followed, which is exactly the kind of follow-up you would expect once a lab-only cycle had been shown to be gameable. The second thing that carries forward is the analytical habit I want you to leave with, independent of cars or emissions entirely: whenever a system is being measured, ask whether that system can tell it is being measured, and whether its behavior might change as a result. That question applies to a diesel engine's control software exactly as much as it applies to a student being observed in a classroom study, a server being benchmarked, or a model being evaluated on a known test set. VW's engineers exploited a version of that gap deliberately. Most of the time nobody is exploiting it on purpose — but the gap itself is not rare, and a completely accurate reading under test conditions is never, by itself, evidence about ordinary conditions.

That is the throughline: an anomaly is not an accusation, a benchmark is not the real world, and the difference between selective presentation and outright manipulation is not the accuracy of any single number — it is whether the thing you measured was quietly built to behave differently while you were watching. Thank you.
